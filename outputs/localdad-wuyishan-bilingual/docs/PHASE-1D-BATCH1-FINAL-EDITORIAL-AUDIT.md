## Executive summary

The four articles now work as a coherent first-stage cluster under **Why Wuyishan**:

1. Natural Heritage explains the protected mountain landscape, biodiversity, and ecological research.
2. Tea Culture explains tea as knowledge, skill, social practice, and a relationship with place.
3. Tea Garden moves from heritage context to a conditional visitor-learning context without promising access.
4. Villages introduces living communities and respectful interpretation without treating village life as scenery.

All four remain `draft` with `trustStatus: research-in-progress`. Their core claims have traceable source support within the limits recorded in the source-verification documents. No article requires a return to broad, open-ended research before the next review. Tea Culture and Villages would benefit from a small editorial polish pass before a publication decision.

## 1. Content consistency audit

### Consistent elements

- The tone is calm, explanatory, and visitor-facing rather than promotional.
- Each article opens with **Why this matters**, develops one clear subject, and ends with a conclusion.
- The articles explain Wuyishan through relationships rather than slogans: nature and research, tea and knowledge, gardens and work, villages and landscape.
- Claims are generally conditional or bounded where the evidence is limited. Words such as `can`, `may`, `some`, `documented`, and `if ... permission` are used appropriately.
- The visitor is invited to observe, ask questions, and understand context rather than consume an attraction.
- The writing avoids tourism clichés, superlatives, and unsupported first-person experience.
- Paragraphs are usually short, and lists divide technical ideas into readable units.

### Intentional differences

| Article | Intentional editorial function | Why the difference is appropriate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural Heritage | Scientific and ecological explanation | It requires more defined terminology and a tighter distinction between protected-area evidence and the wider region. |
| Tea Culture | Cultural context and social interpretation | It can discuss tea drinking, knowledge, and transmission, while keeping China-wide UNESCO context separate from Wuyishan-specific research. |
| Tea Garden | Conditional visitor experience | It uses practical language about permission and working landscapes because no specific public garden is being recommended. |
| Villages | Living-heritage and community ethics | It emphasizes privacy, resident agency, and the limits of generalizing from one village or one person's experience. |

### Repeated concepts

The following repetitions create continuity across the cluster, but are frequent enough to watch in the next edit:

- `more than` and `not only` as the main contrast structure;
- `relationship between people and place`;
- `ask better questions`;
- scenery as the starting point before introducing deeper context;
- knowledge and experience as links between landscape and people.

These ideas are strategically consistent, not contradictory. The repetition becomes most noticeable in Tea Culture, where `not only`, `understand`, and `relationship` appear several times. A future line edit could vary the sentence patterns without changing meaning.

### Contradiction check

No direct contradiction was found.

- Tea Culture suggests that a tea experience can include conversation. Tea Garden and Villages say access or interaction must not be assumed. These statements are compatible because Tea Culture describes an already occurring tea context, while the other articles discuss entering workplaces or lived-in communities.
- Natural Heritage connects people with the landscape in general terms. Villages correctly narrows community claims to documented protected-area settings and specific examples.
- Tea Culture discusses processing knowledge; Tea Garden explicitly states that processing evidence does not prove every cultivation practice.

### Possible editorial issues

1. **Repeated contrast language:** The cluster relies heavily on `not only` and `more than`. This is a style issue, not a factual problem.
2. **B1 with specialist vocabulary:** The sentence structure is mostly B1, but words such as `vegetation`, `ecological`, `apprenticeship`, `cultivation`, `heritage transmission`, `identifiable`, and `informed consent` can move individual passages toward B1–B2. Most are explained by context; not all appear in Key Vocabulary.
3. **Tea Culture wording:** `creating a connection with another way of life` is broader and more romantic than the surrounding evidence-led language. It does not create a hard factual contradiction, but it is a candidate for a minor future edit.
4. **Villages source presentation:** Villages contains its source list in both frontmatter and the Markdown body. The current layout avoids rendering both, but the duplicated editorial data can drift. The other three articles use the structured source list only.
5. **Short descriptions:** Tea Garden and Villages have valid but minimally informative meta descriptions. They do not fully summarize the page's visitor purpose.

## 2. Source audit

### Audit method

- Compared every frontmatter source with the corresponding Phase 1D source-verification document.
- Checked source titles, publishers, URLs or DOIs, and access dates.
- Rechecked all 20 unique source URLs. No source returned a not-found response. Several scholarly and institutional pages returned automated-access responses such as 302, 403, or 418; their DOI or institutional records remain traceable and were already inspected during source verification.
- Compared current wording with the scope reductions applied after verification.

### Natural Heritage

**Source status: Verified**

Sources are traceable and serve distinct roles:

- UNESCO Mount Wuyi supports World Heritage property context, subtropical forest, biodiversity, and monitoring at property level.
- The park introduction supports national-park vegetation zones, environmental gradients, and survey history.
- The Ministry of Ecology and Environment supports national-park biodiversity investigation and research activity.
- The *Forests* paper supports woody-plant and forest-community differences across elevation in a defined reserve and plot system.

**Source overreach:** No unresolved overreach was found in the priority claims. The article now limits elevation claims to vegetation and forest communities and makes research language Wuyishan-specific. The general forest explanation and the human-landscape paragraph are plain-language context rather than findings from the elevation paper; they should not later be expanded into detailed scientific or historical claims without additional mapping.

### Tea Culture

**Source status: Verified**

- UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage supports China-wide tea processing knowledge, drinking, sharing, social practices, and transmission. The article identifies that national scope and says practices vary.
- Kunbing Xiao's *Journal of Material Culture* article supports the Mount Wuyi relationship among terrain, material qualities, specialist skills, embodied knowledge, taste, and fragrance. Verification remains limited to the publisher abstract where full text was unavailable.
- The Fujian safeguarding report supports organized transmission, training, education, and exchange in Wuyishan.
- DB35/T 2157-2023 supports the existence and scope of a current traditional Wuyi Rock Tea processing standard, including safeguarding and transmission.

**Source overreach:** No central source is used beyond its recorded scope. UNESCO is not used as proof that every social practice is a Wuyishan custom. The remaining phrase about `another way of life` is an editorial interpretation rather than a sourced cultural finding and deserves minor attention.

### Tea Garden

**Source status: Verified**

- The Xiao study supplies bounded northern-Fujian and Mount Wuyi context for terrain, tea, specialist knowledge, and skill.
- The soil and intercropping studies support particular seasonal, planting-pattern, soil, and site-condition findings; the article avoids treating them as universal garden conditions.
- China Meteorological Administration supports local monitoring of weather, plant growth, and selected site conditions, but not one universal management routine.
- UNESCO and DB35/T 2157-2023 support heritage and processing knowledge, not garden-access rules.

**Source overreach:** None in the current visitor-access wording. The article explicitly states that no specific garden is identified as open and that any real visit requires confirmed permission. Visitor-boundary advice is correctly presented as general guidance, not a Wuyishan-wide regulation.

### Villages

**Source status: Verified**

- UNESCO Mount Wuyi and the biosphere-reserve page support inhabited protected-area contexts, communities, and selected livelihood information within their stated boundaries.
- The *Land* and *Natural Resources Forum* studies support resident perspectives, tea-farming livelihoods, and participation in conservation within their study populations.
- The cultural-landscape paper supports landscape-level associations involving river systems and topography.
- The Xiamei approval provides one traceable settlement and tea-trade example.
- National-park regulation and zoning sources support resident living, production, planning, and governance context.
- The tea-industry paper supports change in a defined industry, not village life as a whole.

**Source overreach:** None in the current central claims. The article uses `some documented communities`, names Xiamei as one example, and does not generalize from tea farmers to every resident. Privacy, photography, and interaction points are labeled as general respect principles rather than official rules.

**Presentation attention:** The body-level source section duplicates the structured frontmatter source records. This is not evidence overreach, but it is a maintenance risk.

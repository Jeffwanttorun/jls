# English Content Style Guide

This is a permanent editorial standard for all future English website copy, metadata, articles, place pages, interviews, video descriptions, transcripts, and scripts. Read it together with `CONTENT-GUIDE.md`, which contains Jeff's established voice and prohibited brand language.

## Core principle

English content is not Chinese content converted sentence by sentence. The website is not an English translation of a tourism website. It should communicate Wuyishan directly to international visitors in clear, authentic American English.

Preserve the meaning, cultural context, evidence, and Jeff's personality. Change the wording, order, examples, or amount of explanation whenever a literal translation would sound unnatural or leave an international reader without necessary context.

Before keeping a sentence, ask:

> Would an American English speaker naturally explain this idea this way to someone who has never been to Wuyishan?

If not, rewrite it as English communication rather than polishing the translation.

## Voice

The desired voice is a knowledgeable person who lives in Wuyishan sharing a meaningful place with visitors. Use a conversational documentary style: grounded in real places and people, interested in context, and calm enough to let the evidence carry the story.

The writing should be:

- natural American English;
- clear to readers who know nothing about Wuyishan;
- personal when Jeff's confirmed experience adds useful context, but never centered on Jeff for its own sake;
- informative without sounding like a lecture or research paper;
- respectful, specific, and evidence-based;
- easy for an international reader to understand and for Jeff to say aloud.

Use academic language only when a scientific, historical, or cultural subject genuinely requires it. Define specialist terms in plain English at first use.

## Explain; do not promote

When introducing Wuyishan, focus on:

- why the place matters;
- how landscape, culture, tea, and communities connect;
- verified history and real human experience;
- specific observations rather than abstract praise;
- what is known, who knows it, and what still needs confirmation.

Do not tell readers that Wuyishan is important merely by praising it. Give them the context to understand its importance themselves.

Avoid:

- literal translations and Chinese sentence order;
- government-brochure, official-notice, or ceremonial language;
- tourism advertising and promotional calls to action;
- unsupported superlatives or exaggerated global claims;
- empty emotional phrases that could describe any destination;
- machine-translation phrasing;
- academic distance when plain language would be more useful;
- generic AI travel language.

## Keep each content type focused

Content types may link to one another, but each page should have one clear editorial job.

| Content type | Primary job |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge article | Explain significance, evidence, and historical or cultural context. |
| Place page | Help a reader understand one real location and its verified practical context. |
| Story or interview | Show a real human experience and how it connects with a larger Wuyishan theme. |
| Video | Extend understanding through verified visual, spoken, or interview material. |
| Guide | Provide practical travel information with dates and verification status where details can change. |

A knowledge article should not become an itinerary. A place page should not make broad historical claims without sources. A story should not stand in for evidence about an entire community. A video should not be treated as proof merely because it shows a place. A practical guide should not use promotion in place of useful detail.

## First-person voice

Use first person when it identifies the source of a real observation, explains a choice, or helps a reader understand Jeff's relationship with Wuyishan. Do not add first-person language merely to make neutral information sound personal.

When it accurately reflects Jeff's experience, a natural direction is:

> Living in Wuyishan has shown me that the place matters for more than its scenery.

Avoid self-promotional introductions such as:

> I will introduce the world's most amazing mountain.

Jeff is the connection point between the reader and Wuyishan, not the main subject of every page. `Local Dad Jeff` remains the brand name; normal sentences should introduce him as a dad, coffee-cart owner, or person living in Wuyishan when that context is relevant.

## Cultural adaptation

Do not preserve a Chinese identity label, idiom, institutional phrase, metaphor, or internet expression simply because it is familiar in Chinese. Decide what the expression is doing in context, then choose the natural English way to do the same job.

When no direct English equivalent exists:

1. identify the meaning an international reader actually needs;
2. choose the closest natural English expression;
3. add a short explanation when cultural context would otherwise be lost;
4. retain a Chinese term only when the term itself matters, and explain it at first use;
5. never guess a translation, romanization, title, or cultural relationship.

## Accuracy and evidence

Never invent historical details, scientific claims, cultural relationships, quotations, personal stories, field observations, or international connections.

Important claims need an identifiable source or a clear verification status. Keep Jeff's firsthand experience separate from scholarly, historical, scientific, and official information. If evidence is incomplete, narrow the claim, label the uncertainty, or leave it unpublished.

Natural English does not justify changing the factual meaning. Editing may clarify or reorganize a claim, but it must not make the claim broader, stronger, or more certain than the source supports.

## Write for speaking as well as reading

The English website should help Jeff build language he can understand, say, and reuse in YouTube videos, conversations with visitors, and future English guiding work.

Prefer sentences with a clear subject, familiar verbs, and a natural spoken rhythm. Read important passages aloud. If a sentence is difficult to say, packed with abstract nouns, or sounds memorized from a textbook, simplify it without removing necessary meaning.

This does not mean making every subject casual. Scientific, historical, and cultural topics can remain precise while using direct sentences and plain explanations.

## Editorial check before publication

Ask:

1. Does this sound written in English, or translated into English?
2. Would a first-time international reader understand the necessary Wuyishan context?
3. Is the page doing the job of its content type?
4. Does every personal statement come from Jeff's confirmed experience?
5. Are important claims sourced or clearly marked for verification?
6. Has natural wording accidentally made any claim stronger than its evidence?
7. Could Jeff comfortably say this sentence in a video or conversation?
8. Does any sentence sound like a brochure, official notice, advertisement, textbook, or generic AI copy?

Revise until the answer is clear.

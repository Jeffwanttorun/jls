**Wuyi rock tea** is a style of oolong from the Wuyi Mountains. In Chinese, this category is known as *yan cha*. English speakers usually call it Wuyi rock tea. The tea bushes grow among cliffs and weathered rock, and the finished leaves are usually roasted. Tea isn’t just a souvenir here; it’s part of work, family life, and everyday hospitality.

## What does it taste like?

There’s no single rock-tea flavor. One cup may be floral and bright; another may taste dark, toasty, fruity, spicy, or woody. Tea drinkers often use the term *yan yun* for a lingering mineral character associated with Wuyi rock tea. There isn’t an exact English equivalent. Don’t worry about identifying it right away. Start with a much easier question: Do I want another cup?

## Three names you’ll hear a lot

### Da Hong Pao

**Da Hong Pao** is the best-known name in Wuyi tea. The original mother bushes are protected and are not the source of the Da Hong Pao sold in everyday shops. Today, the name is used for many commercial teas, often blends made to create a balanced Wuyi rock-tea style.

### Rou Gui

Rou Gui is known for a strong fragrance that people sometimes compare to cinnamon or warm spice. The flavor can still vary a lot depending on where it was grown and how it was roasted.

### Shui Xian

Shui Xian often has a softer, deeper character. Older tea bushes and careful roasting can produce rich, smooth cups.

## How to enjoy a tea visit

At many tea shops, you can sit down and taste a few teas before buying. You don’t have to pretend you know more than you do. Simple words work fine: light, strong, floral, roasted, or smooth.

## Buying tea without feeling pressured

Set a budget and take your time. A higher price doesn’t always mean you’ll like the tea more. Ask what kind of tea it is, where it was grown, how heavily it was roasted, and when it was made. If you feel rushed or pressured, it’s fine to say thanks and try another shop.

> Tea is part of everyday hospitality here. The best first step is simply to sit down and have a cup.
